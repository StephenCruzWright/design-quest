# Learning science for Design Quest: a literature review

## Abstract

This review summarises the experimental and meta-analytic evidence behind the main design decisions in Design Quest, a browser game in which players read short lesson pages with interactive demos, pass a judgement quiz, then repair broken client pages with real CSS while automatic checks measure the rendered result. It covers retrieval practice, spacing and interleaving, desirable difficulties, feedback, cognitive load and worked examples, multimedia design and animation, mastery learning, productive failure, self-explanation, game-based learning, perceptual learning and help-seeking in tutoring systems. The strongest and most consistent findings are that retrieval with corrective feedback beats restudy (g = 0.50 overall, g = 0.73 when feedback is given), that interleaving helps category learning of visual material (g = 0.67 for paintings), that elaborated feedback beats right-or-wrong feedback, that prompted self-explanation helps (g = 0.55), and that interesting but irrelevant additions hurt learning (g = -0.33). Game-based instruction outperforms conventional instruction by a small margin (d = 0.29 to 0.33) and is not reliably more motivating; entertainment value does not predict learning. The review ends with a numbered list of decisions for the game: lesson gates designed as production-style retrieval with explanatory feedback, interleaved quizzes and arcade rounds, the written rationale as a self-explanation prompt, animation restricted to showing change the player controls, no ambient decoration during reading, and a hint ladder that never ends in the answer.

## Scope and method

Every source cited here was located and checked during the preparation of this review. Effect sizes are reported as the source reports them: Cohen's d or Hedges' g (standardised mean differences, where 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large) or r. Where a source gives sample sizes or the number of effect sizes (k), they are included. Laboratory findings with word lists or prose passages do not transfer automatically to a game about visual design, so each section separates what the evidence shows from what it implies for Design Quest.

The game's structure, for reference: a level (`LevelDef`, "Desk" in the UI) contains a lesson of about six pages with slider demos, a five-question quiz drawn from a pool of twelve, and three client challenges (`BossDef`) in which the player edits CSS while judge functions measure the rendered DOM and return `CheckResult` objects with plain-language explanations. The player writes a one or two sentence rationale before shipping a fix. Hints are progressive. Under consideration: a gate at the end of every lesson page, more animation, a player sprite, "juice" and a rule that every line of copy is story or fact.

## 1. Retrieval practice and the testing effect

Roediger and Karpicke [1] had 120 undergraduates (Experiment 1) study prose passages and then either restudy them or take a free-recall test without feedback. On a final test 5 minutes later restudy won (81% vs 75% of idea units, d = 0.52). After 2 days the tested group recalled 68% against 54% (d = 0.95), and after 1 week 56% against 42% (d = 0.83). In Experiment 2 (180 students), the group that studied once and took three tests recalled 61% after a week, against 40% for the group that studied four times (d = 1.26), yet the repeat-study group was the most confident that it would remember the passage. Two points matter for design: the benefit of testing appears on delayed tests and can reverse on immediate ones, and learners' confidence tracks fluency, not retention.

Karpicke and Blunt [2] compared retrieval practice with concept mapping, an elaborative study activity, across two studies with about 200 students learning science texts. Retrieval practice produced roughly 50% better long-term retention, the advantage held for inference questions, and it held even when the final test required building a concept map. Students predicted the opposite ordering.

Rowland's meta-analysis [3] pooled 159 effect sizes from 61 studies (1975 to 2013) that compared testing with restudy. The mean effect was g = 0.50 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.58), with high heterogeneity (I² = 84). The moderators bear directly on lesson gates:

- Feedback: studies that gave corrective feedback after the test produced g = 0.73; studies without feedback produced g = 0.39.
- Retrieval success without feedback: when initial test accuracy was 50% or lower and no feedback was given, the testing effect vanished (g = 0.03). It reached g = 0.29 at 51% to 75% accuracy and g = 0.56 above 75%.
- Test format: in the subset with feedback or high initial accuracy, cued recall (g = 0.72) and free recall (g = 0.81) produced roughly twice the benefit of recognition tests such as multiple choice (g = 0.36).
- Delay: effects were larger when the final test came a day or more after learning.
- Feedback timing: delayed feedback (g = 1.38, k = 6) outperformed immediate feedback (g = 0.66, k = 46), a result Rowland flags as fragile because of the small k and a confound with retention interval.
- Publication bias: unpublished studies showed smaller effects, mostly because they tended to lack feedback and have low initial accuracy.

Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan [4] meta-analysed 272 effect sizes from 118 articles with more than 15,000 participants and found practice tests beat restudy and every other comparison condition, with an overall effect of g = 0.61; effects were moderated by test format, participant characteristics and study design. Pan and Rickard [5] asked whether testing helps on different questions from the ones practised. Across their transfer meta-analysis the mean effect was d = 0.40. When the response required on the final test matched the practised response (response congruency), the effect rose to d = 0.58; without congruency it was d = 0.28. Elaborated retrieval practice, including elaborative feedback, added about d = 0.23. Dunlosky and colleagues [6] reviewed ten common study techniques and gave only practice testing and distributed practice a high utility rating; rereading, highlighting and summarisation were rated low.

**Implications.** A gate at the end of a lesson page is retrieval practice, and the evidence supports it on three conditions. It must give corrective, explanatory feedback, because retrieval without feedback at low success rates produces no benefit [3]. It should require production (set a value, or drag to a target) more than recognition, because recall-type tests roughly double the effect of recognition tests [3]. Its response format should resemble what the player later does in the boss fight, because response congruency drives transfer [5]. Immediate measures (page completion speed, satisfaction) will understate the benefit and may favour plain rereading [1].

## 2. Spacing and interleaving

Cepeda and colleagues [7] synthesised 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317 experiments in 184 articles. Spaced study beat massed study, and the gap between study sessions that maximised retention grew as the retention interval grew: material needed for longer requires wider spacing. Latimier, Peyre and Ramus [11] meta-analysed spaced retrieval practice specifically and confirmed that spacing retrieval episodes beats massing them and that combining spacing with retrieval beats either alone. They found no clear evidence that expanding schedules (gaps that lengthen each time) outperform uniform ones.

Interleaving mixes different kinds of problems or categories within a practice session. Rohrer and Taylor [8] found that college students who practised mathematics problems spaced across sessions, or mixed by problem type rather than blocked, performed much better one week later. Kornell and Bjork [9] tested interleaving on a task close to design judgement: learning to recognise painters' styles. Participants (120 in Experiment 1a, 72 in 1b) saw paintings by 12 artists either blocked by artist or interleaved. On new paintings, accuracy was .61 after interleaved study and .35 after blocked study in Experiment 1a. Across Experiments 1a and 2, 85% of participants did at least as well with interleaving, but 83% rated blocking as equally or more effective.

Brunmair and Richter [10] meta-analysed 238 effect sizes from 59 studies. The overall interleaving effect was g = 0.42, largest for paintings (g = 0.67) and other visual materials, smaller for mathematics (g = 0.34), not significant for expository texts, and reversed for word learning, where blocking won (g = -0.39). Meta-regression showed larger interleaving benefits when categories were similar to each other and when items within a category varied, and for more complex material. The heterogeneity was substantial: interleaving is not a universal default.

**Implications.** Design judgement is inductive category learning over visual material, the condition where interleaving has its largest measured effect [9, 10]. Quizzes and arcade rounds should mix items from earlier levels, and should place confusable categories side by side (a spacing problem next to an alignment problem that looks similar). Pure vocabulary items (what a term means) are the one case where blocked practice tested better [10]. Revisiting earlier levels at widening intervals is supported, but a simple uniform review rhythm is as good as an elaborate expanding schedule [11]. Players will prefer blocked practice and judge it more effective [9], so player preference is not evidence about learning.

## 3. Desirable difficulties, the generation effect and pretesting

Bjork [12] introduced the term "desirable difficulties" for manipulations that slow apparent progress during training but improve long-term retention and transfer, including varying the conditions of practice, interleaving tasks, spacing sessions, testing and reducing the frequency of feedback. Bjork and Bjork [13] separate performance during learning from learning itself and argue that learners' everyday experience does not give them an accurate model of which activities produce learning. The difficulty is desirable only when the learner can respond to it successfully; a task the learner cannot complete teaches little, which matches Rowland's finding that low-success retrieval without feedback gives no benefit [3].

The generation effect is the finding that producing information yields better memory than reading it. Bertsch and colleagues [14] meta-analysed 445 effect sizes from 86 studies and found a mean advantage of about 0.40 standard deviations, with substantial variability across moderators.

Pretesting means asking questions before the learner has studied the answer. Richland, Kornell and Kao [15] ran five experiments in which students answered questions about an essay on vision before reading it. Pretested students learned the tested concepts better than students given extra study time, even though most pretest answers were wrong, and the advantage survived controls in which the tested concepts were italicised or bolded for the extended-study group. The benefit therefore does not reduce to attention cueing.

**Implications.** A lesson page can open with the question and close with the gate: asking first (pretest) and asking again after the explanation (retrieval) are two separate supported mechanisms. Demos in which the player predicts what a slider position will do before dragging it are generation tasks. Each difficulty must stay within reach: a gate that most players fail repeatedly has crossed from desirable to undesirable [3, 13].

## 4. Feedback

Feedback has one of the largest average effects in education and one of the widest spreads. Kluger and DeNisi [18] analysed 607 effect sizes from 23,663 observations. The mean effect was d = 0.41, but more than a third of feedback interventions reduced performance, and this could not be explained by sampling error or by whether the feedback was positive or negative. Their feedback intervention theory holds that feedback shifts attention among task details, task motivation and the self; the further attention moves towards the self, the weaker or more harmful the effect. Praise and feedback that threatened self-esteem were among the moderators that reduced effects [17].

Hattie and Timperley [16] synthesised 12 meta-analyses covering 196 studies and 6,972 effect sizes, with an average effect of 0.79. In their model, feedback answers three questions ("Where am I going?", "How am I going?", "Where to next?") at four levels: the task, the process used to do the task, self-regulation, and the self. Process-level feedback was more effective than task-level feedback for deeper learning; task-level feedback often does not generalise to other tasks; praise, rewards and punishment had the lowest effects; and mixing praise with corrective information diluted the correction. Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie [20] updated this with 435 studies, 994 effect sizes and more than 61,000 participants. The mean was d = 0.48 with large heterogeneity, and information content was the main moderator: reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24 (k = 39), corrective feedback d = 0.46 (k = 238), high-information feedback d = 0.99 (k = 42). Effects on cognitive outcomes (d = 0.51) were larger than on motivation (d = 0.33).

Van der Kleij, Feskens and Eggen [19] meta-analysed 70 effect sizes from 40 studies of feedback in computer-based environments. Elaborated feedback that explained the answer produced 0.49, giving the correct answer produced 0.32, and saying only right or wrong produced 0.05. Elaborated feedback was especially effective for higher-order outcomes.

Shute's review [17] turns this into guidelines. Things to do: focus feedback on the task, not the learner; give elaborated feedback that addresses what, how and why; present it in manageable units, because too much at once causes superficial processing and overload; be specific; give feedback only after the learner has attempted the problem. Things to avoid: normative comparisons with other learners; overall grades without comments; feedback that threatens self-esteem; praise ("sparingly, if at all", because it directs attention to the self); interrupting a learner who is actively working; and "progressive hints that always terminate with the correct answer", because hints are abused unless the design prevents it.

On timing, the evidence conflicts. Kulik and Kulik's meta-analysis of 53 studies [21] is cited by Shute [17] as showing that classroom (field) studies tend to favour immediate feedback while laboratory studies of list learning tend to favour delay. Butler, Karpicke and Roediger [22] found that delayed feedback after a multiple-choice test produced better performance on a delayed cued-recall test than immediate feedback, and that answer-until-correct feedback produced the same learning as being shown the correct answer. Van der Kleij and colleagues [19] found delayed timing associated with smaller effects in computer-based settings. Shute [17] reconciles these by task and learner: immediate feedback helps novices, low-achieving learners and procedural skills, while delayed feedback can favour transfer for more capable learners.

**Implications.** The judges already return plain-language explanations; that is high-information feedback, the category with the largest effect [20]. Quiz and gate feedback should explain why the wrong option fails and which principle applies, not only mark it wrong [19]. Celebration on success is reinforcement, the weakest category (d = 0.24) [20], and praise aimed at the player ("You're a natural!") moves attention to the self [16, 18]. Success feedback should instead state what the check measured. For novices working on CSS, immediate feedback on submission is appropriate [17]; for review modes, showing the explanation at the end of a round is supported by delayed-feedback findings [3, 22]. Leaderboards or percentile comparisons are normative feedback and are on Shute's avoid list [17].

## 5. Cognitive load, worked examples and the expertise reversal effect

Sweller [23] argued that conventional problem solving by means-ends analysis consumes working memory that would otherwise go to schema acquisition, so novices can solve problems without learning their structure. Studying worked examples reduces that load. Atkinson, Derry, Renkl and Wortham [24] reviewed the worked-example literature and derived design principles: examples work best when their parts are integrated, when they use more than one modality, and when their conceptual structure is made visible by labelling or segmenting the steps. Renkl and Atkinson [26] showed that examples are most effective early in skill acquisition and problem solving is better later, and proposed fading: removing worked steps one at a time until the learner solves complete problems. Renkl [27] later integrated worked examples, observational learning and analogical reasoning into one instructional theory of example-based learning.

Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller [25] named the expertise reversal effect: techniques that help novices lose their benefit, and can become harmful, for learners with more prior knowledge, because redundant guidance has to be processed against existing schemas. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark [28] argued from cognitive architecture and half a century of studies that minimally guided instruction is less effective and efficient than guided instruction for novices, with the advantage of guidance receding only when learners have enough prior knowledge to guide themselves. Alfieri and colleagues' meta-analysis [29] of 164 studies supports a qualified version: unassisted discovery was worse than explicit instruction (d = -0.38), while enhanced discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations beat other forms of instruction (d = 0.30).

**Implications.** The first client in each level should be preceded by an annotated worked example: a broken page, the fix and the measured result, with each step labelled by the principle it applies [24]. The three clients in a level can implement fading: the first client arrives with part of the CSS fix written and the player completes it, the third arrives with nothing [26]. Returning players and later levels need less of this; the game should let a player who passes the gate first time skip the extended worked example rather than force every player through it [25]. Open-ended CSS editing without examples or checks is the unassisted discovery condition that tested worst [29].

## 6. Multimedia learning, seductive details and animation

Mayer and Moreno [30] ground multimedia design in three assumptions: separate channels for pictures and words, limited capacity in each, and learning as active selection, organisation and integration. From these come principles for reducing extraneous processing, chief among them coherence (exclude extraneous words, pictures and sounds) and signalling (highlight the essential material).

The seductive details literature tests coherence directly. Harp and Mayer [31] added interesting but irrelevant illustrated anecdotes (for example about people struck by lightning) to a lesson on how lightning forms; recall and problem-solving transfer both fell, and their experiments indicated that seductive details do damage by priming the wrong prior knowledge, so learners organise the lesson around the anecdotes. Rey [32] meta-analysed 39 experimental effects and found a significant seductive detail effect, small to medium on retention and medium on transfer, larger when study time was limited. Sundararajan and Adesope [33] meta-analysed 68 effects from 58 studies with more than 7,500 students and found g = -0.33. The effect was larger when details were static images, when they combined text and image, when learners could not control the pace, and when details were frequent or used the same format as the core content. It was smaller when learners were warned the material was irrelevant or had strong prior knowledge.

Signalling has a smaller, positive effect. Richter, Scheiter and Eitel [34] meta-analysed 45 comparisons from 27 studies (N = 2,464) of signals that link text to pictures, such as colour coding, and found r = 0.17 for comprehension and transfer, moderated by prior knowledge: signals helped low prior-knowledge learners most.

Animation is not a single thing. Höffler and Leutner [35] meta-analysed 76 comparisons from 26 studies of instructional animation against static pictures and found a mean advantage of d = 0.37 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.49). Representational animation, which depicts the content itself, produced d = 0.40; video-based realistic animation produced d = 0.76; and animation teaching procedural-motor knowledge produced d = 1.06. Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt [36] reviewed earlier claims and found that many apparent advantages of animation came from comparisons where the animated version carried more information than the static one or included interactivity or prediction, both of which help on their own. They argue from a congruence principle (a graphic should match the structure of the concept, so animation suits concepts about change over time) and an apprehension principle (the graphic must be perceived accurately). Animations are often too fast or complex to perceive, and many continuous events are understood as discrete steps; they suggest interactivity as a remedy.

"Juice" is a game-design term for redundant, amplified feedback in which one player action triggers several non-functional reactions. Hicks, Gerling, Dickinson and Vanden Abeele [37] define it as a form of visual embellishment and note that the industry belief that it improves player experience lacked empirical support. Kao and colleagues [38] ran a pre-registered online experiment with 1,699 participants that separated three properties of juicy feedback. Feedback that depended on success raised every motive measured (effectance, competence and curiosity). Amplification on its own lowered them, which the authors attribute to reduced sense of agency. Curiosity was the strongest predictor of enjoyment and the only predictor of playtime.

**Implications.** The slider demos are the animation the evidence supports: the layout change is the content (representational), the player controls it (interactive), and it depicts change, which satisfies the congruence principle [35, 36]. A sprite that idles, blinks or walks while the player reads a lesson page is a static or looping seductive detail with no pacing control, the configuration with the largest measured harm [33]. Signalling should be used inside demos, for example colour-linking the measured gap in the demo to the number in the explanation, which helps the novices this game targets [34]. Juice should fire only on real success and should point at what succeeded (the gap that now measures correctly), not amplify every click [38]. "Every line is story or fact" agrees with the coherence principle as long as story means the client's brief and the reason the fix matters; anecdotes that do not carry the principle are seductive details by definition [31, 33].

## 7. Mastery learning and gating

Bloom [39] reported that students tutored one-to-one with mastery-learning techniques scored about two standard deviations above conventionally taught classes, and posed the "2 sigma problem" of matching this with group instruction. The figure came from two dissertations in which students were taught unfamiliar topics (probability, cartography) over about three weeks and tested immediately on narrow, researcher-made tests. Von Hippel [40] reviews the later evidence: tutoring meta-analyses average 0.33 standard deviations (Cohen, Kulik and Kulik, 65 studies) and 0.37 (Nickow and colleagues, 96 randomised studies, none reaching two sigma); mastery learning effects average 0.84 on narrow tests but 0.27 on standardised ones; and in Bloom's own data mastery learning without tutoring produced about 1.1 sigma, so roughly half the headline figure came from frequent testing, feedback and retesting rather than tutoring.

Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns [41] meta-analysed 108 controlled evaluations of mastery learning programmes and found a mean effect of 0.52 on examinations. Effects were stronger for weaker students, varied with the mastery procedures used, and came with more time spent on instruction. VanLehn [42] compared tutoring types against no tutoring: answer-based computer tutors that check only final answers produced 0.31, step-based intelligent tutors that respond to each step produced 0.76, and human tutors 0.79. The granularity of the interaction, not the human, accounts for most of the difference.

**Implications.** A gate that blocks the next page until the current one is understood is a mastery criterion, and mastery programmes have a reliable moderate effect, largest for the weakest learners [41]. The Bloom figure should not be used to justify it [40]. Two cautions follow. A single correct multiple-choice answer is a weak criterion of mastery, since a player can guess through it, so gates need either a production response or a fresh item after a miss. Mastery also costs time [41], so gates should be short. The judges that check each property of the rendered page are closer to a step-based tutor than an answer-based one, which is the granularity associated with the larger effect [42].

## 8. Productive failure and invention

Kapur [43] had student groups work on complex, ill-structured problems before receiving instruction. They produced poor solutions during the problem-solving phase but outperformed students taught first on later tests. Kapur [44] later framed four outcomes: productive failure, productive success, unproductive failure and unproductive success, arguing that initial failure is productive only when the activity activates relevant prior knowledge and the subsequent instruction builds on the learners' attempts. Schwartz and Martin [46] found that students who invented their own statistical methods before instruction were better prepared to learn from that instruction, an advantage that did not appear on immediate efficiency measures. Schwartz and Bransford [47] had students analyse contrasting cases of experimental data and invent graphs before a lecture; they learned and transferred more from the same lecture than students who read and summarised a chapter first. Their claim is that there is "a time for telling", and that telling works best after the learner has noticed what needs explaining.

Sinha and Kapur [45] meta-analysed 166 comparisons from 53 studies of problem solving followed by instruction against instruction followed by problem solving. The overall effect favoured problem solving first, g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51), rising to g = 0.37 to 0.58 for designs with high fidelity to productive-failure principles, and to an estimated g = 0.87 after correction for publication bias. The pattern reversed for younger learners (grades 2 to 5) and for domain-general skills, where instruction first was better. This sits in tension with the guidance arguments in Section 5 [28]: the resolution in the literature is that the initial problem must be designed so the learner can generate attempts using what they know, and full instruction must follow.

**Implications.** A short "try it first" step at the start of a lesson page (drag the slider to where the card reads well, before any explanation) is a small, low-cost version of problem solving first, and the explanation can then refer to the attempts the player made [43, 47]. This supports placing the demo before the text on some pages. It does not support sending players into a boss fight before the lesson: the CSS repair is a complex procedural task where novices need worked examples [24, 28], and the meta-analysis does not cover game players working alone without a teacher [45].

## 9. Self-explanation

Chi and colleagues [48] analysed think-aloud protocols of students studying worked mechanics examples. Successful students generated many explanations that tied each solution step to principles from the text and monitored their own understanding accurately; less successful students explained little and later relied on copying examples. Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu and LaVancher [49] then prompted 14 eighth-graders to self-explain after each line of a text on the circulatory system while 10 controls read it twice. The prompted group gained more, and high explainers in the prompted group all reached the correct mental model.

Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi and Winne [50] meta-analysed 69 effect sizes from 64 reports (about 6,000 participants) of prompted self-explanation and found g = 0.55. Self-explanation beat comparison activities including instructor-provided explanations, note-taking, summarising and additional practice. Prompts that asked learners to choose an explanation from a menu were the least effective form, self-explanations about one's own knowledge rather than the material were less effective, and the authors suggest that as knowledge grows, learners benefit from more freedom in what and how they explain. Mayer's review of educational games [54] lists prompts for self-explanation among the five game features with consistent value-added evidence.

**Implications.** The required rationale before shipping a fix is a self-explanation prompt and is supported [50, 54]. It should be free text, not a menu of reasons [50]. It should ask the player to connect the change to the principle ("Which rule does this change apply, and what did it change on the page?") rather than to rate their confidence, which is metacognitive and less effective [50]. Showing the reference rationale after submission turns the step into a comparison the player can check their reasoning against. Early levels can use a sentence frame; later levels can drop it.

## 10. Game-based learning and gamification

Wouters and colleagues [51] meta-analysed serious games against conventional instruction: 77 studies (5,547 participants) for learning and 31 (2,216) for motivation. Games were more effective for learning (d = 0.29) and retention (d = 0.36) but not more motivating (d = 0.26, not significant). Games worked better when supplemented with other instruction, when play spanned multiple sessions and when players worked in groups. Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth [52] found digital games beat non-game conditions for K-16 learners (d = 0.33, k = 57) and that augmented game designs beat standard versions of the same game (d = 0.34, k = 20); effects varied with game mechanics, visual and narrative characteristics and study quality. Sitzmann [53] meta-analysed 65 studies (6,476 trainees) of computer-based simulation games: declarative knowledge was 11% higher, procedural knowledge 14% higher, retention 9% higher and self-efficacy 20% higher than in comparison groups. Games helped when they were a supplement, when they conveyed material actively, and when learners could replay without limit; as standalone instruction they lost to the comparison group. Games rated high in entertainment value taught no more than games rated low.

Mayer [54] divides game research into value-added studies (one feature added to a base game), cognitive-consequences studies (whether playing off-the-shelf games improves general cognitive skills) and media comparisons. The value-added studies support five features: modality (spoken rather than printed words), personalisation (conversational style), pretraining, coaching (advice at key points) and self-explanation prompts. Cognitive-consequences research does not support claims of broad transfer. Plass, Homer and Kinzer [55] argue that game-based learning has to be designed for cognitive, motivational, affective and sociocultural engagement together, since a game element that raises engagement on one dimension can add load on another.

Sailer and Homner [56] meta-analysed gamification (game elements added to non-game instruction) and found effects on cognitive outcomes (g = 0.49, k = 19, N = 1,686), motivational outcomes (g = 0.36, k = 16, N = 2,246) and behavioural outcomes (g = 0.25, k = 9, N = 951). Only the cognitive effect remained stable when the analysis was restricted to methodologically rigorous studies.

**Implications.** The game format has a small, reliable learning advantage and no reliable motivational one [51, 53]. Fun added on top does not produce learning [53]; the learning comes from the features that are themselves learning mechanisms (feedback, self-explanation, coaching, retrieval), which Design Quest already has [54]. Unlimited replay of challenges is supported [53]. A conversational, second-person voice in copy is supported by the personalisation finding [54]; spending effort on a sprite or ambient effects is not supported by any of these meta-analyses, and the seductive detail evidence counts against it during reading [33].

## 11. Perceptual learning and contrasting cases

Kellman, Massey and Son [57] define perceptual learning as improvement in extracting information from displays through experience, with two components: discovering which features matter, and fluency (faster, less effortful, more parallel extraction). Their perceptual learning modules present many short classification or matching trials with feedback, across varied examples, rather than a few worked problems. In a module on mapping between graphs, equations and word problems, 68 ninth and tenth graders improved more than controls who practised conventional problems. In an algebraic transformations module, 30 students who were already about 80% accurate cut their solution time from about 28 to about 12 seconds per problem, with gains held at a two-week delayed test. A linear measurement module with 63 sixth graders produced gains on measurement and fraction problems that were unchanged at a four-month delayed test. A later randomised efficacy trial of four modules with two cohorts of sixth graders found consistent gains for linear and area measurement modules, mixed results for fraction modules, and significant effects on a composite test given a year after the intervention [58].

Contrasting cases are sets of examples that differ in a few chosen features so that learners notice what matters. In Schwartz and Bransford's study [47], analysing contrasting cases prepared students to learn from a later lecture.

**Implications.** Judging whether a layout's spacing, hierarchy or contrast works is a perceptual classification skill, and the module format (many fast items, immediate feedback, varied examples, interleaved categories) matches it [57]. An arcade mode of rapid "which of these two is better, and why" items drawn from all completed levels combines perceptual learning, interleaving [10] and retrieval [3]. Before-and-after pairs should differ in one property at a time (only the leading changes, only the measure changes) so they work as contrasting cases [47]. The mixed fraction results [58] are a reminder that the format does not work automatically for every concept.

## 12. Hints and help-seeking

Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer and Wallace [59] reviewed help use in interactive learning environments and found that learners often use on-demand help badly: some avoid it when they need it, others overuse it. Aleven, McLaren, Roll and Koedinger [62] built a model of good help-seeking for the Geometry Cognitive Tutor and found students asking for help too often or too rarely. Roll, Aleven, McLaren and Koedinger [63] added a Help Tutor that gave immediate feedback on help-seeking errors. In two studies (58 and 67 students) it improved help-seeking behaviour, and in the second study the improved help-seeking carried over to learning new content in the following month, after the support was removed.

Baker, Corbett, Koedinger and Wagner [61] observed students using Cognitive Tutors and identified "gaming the system": exploiting regularities in feedback and help, such as clicking through hints to reach the answer, to advance without thinking. Gaming was associated with reduced learning nearly as strongly as any other off-task behaviour, and its frequency correlated with post-test scores about as strongly as prior knowledge did. The Cognitive Tutor programme itself [60] shows what well-designed step-level help can do at scale: in a deployment with more than 500 students in Pittsburgh city schools, users of the Practical Algebra Tutor scored 100% higher on tests of the targeted curriculum and 15% higher on standardised tests than non-users. Shute's guidelines [17] summarise the design lesson: avoid hint sequences that always end with the answer.

**Implications.** Progressive hints are supported, with constraints. The last hint should point at the property and the principle (for example, "the gap between the heading and its paragraph is larger than the gap above the heading") but should not contain the CSS that fixes it [17, 61]. Rapid hint requests, or hint requests before any edit, are the signature of gaming [61]; a short required attempt before the next hint unlocks, or a nudge to try an edit, addresses this. Players who never open hints while failing repeatedly are the opposite help-seeking error [62]; offering a hint after a number of failed submissions addresses that. Feedback on how the player uses help can itself improve later learning [63].

## 13. Implications for Design Quest

The following decisions are supported or warned against by the evidence above. Numbers in brackets point to the references; strength is stated where it matters.

1. **Keep the lesson gates, and design them as retrieval with feedback.** Retrieval beats restudy (g = 0.50) and nearly doubles with feedback (g = 0.73) [3]. A gate without explanatory feedback, or one most players fail, gives no measurable benefit (g = 0.03 at 50% accuracy or below without feedback) [3]. Every gate answer, right or wrong, shows a one or two sentence explanation tied to the page's principle.

2. **Prefer demo-goal gates to multiple-choice gates.** Recall-type tests roughly double the benefit of recognition tests [3], and transfer is larger when the practised response matches the later task (d = 0.58 against 0.28) [5]. "Set the line height so the paragraph passes" is closer to the boss fight than "Which line height is best?". Where multiple choice is used, each distractor needs its own explanation [19].

3. **Make a wrong gate answer lead to a new item, not a retry of the same one.** Answer-until-correct produced the same learning as being shown the answer [22], so retrying is not harmful in itself, but with four options it lets a player guess through, which turns the gate into the answer-based checking that tested weakest [42] and invites gaming [61]. After a miss: show the explanation, then present a variant of the item.

4. **Calibrate gate difficulty so most players pass on the first or second attempt.** Benefits rise with initial retrieval success [3]; the difficulty is desirable only while the player can meet it [12, 13]. Track first-attempt pass rates per gate and rewrite gates that sit below about half.

5. **Ask before explaining on some pages.** A question or a "try it first" slider at the top of a page, answered properly at the bottom, uses pretesting [15] and a small dose of problem solving first [45, 47]. Keep the full CSS boss fights after instruction, where novices need worked examples [24, 28].

6. **Shuffle question order and answer order in every quiz draw.** Randomisation removes position cues that let players answer from memory of the layout rather than the content, which is a regularity of the kind gaming exploits [61].

7. **Interleave earlier levels into later quizzes and the arcade.** Interleaving is strongest for visual category learning (g = 0.67 for paintings) and for categories that are easy to confuse [9, 10]. A level-five quiz should include items from levels one to four, with confusable principles adjacent. Keep definition-only items blocked within their own level, where blocking tested better [10].

8. **Bring players back at spaced intervals.** Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval [7, 11]; a uniform review rhythm is as good as an expanding one [11]. A daily or per-session arcade round drawn from completed levels is enough.

9. **Do not use player ratings or immediate scores to judge whether a learning feature works.** Restudy wins at 5 minutes and loses at 1 week [1]; players rate blocked practice higher while learning less from it [9]; repeat-readers are the most confident [1]. Judge features by delayed performance, for example arcade accuracy on a later session.

10. **Make the judges' failure messages high-information and task-focused.** High-information feedback (d = 0.99) beats corrective (d = 0.46) and reinforcement (d = 0.24) feedback [20]; elaborated feedback beats right-or-wrong in computer settings (0.49 against 0.05) [19]. Each failing check should name the measured value, the target and the principle, in manageable units: one failing check explained at a time, not a wall of results [17].

11. **Keep praise and celebration short, success-dependent and informative.** Praise and self-directed feedback reduce feedback effects [16, 17, 18]. Juice that amplifies every action lowered competence and curiosity in a 1,699-person experiment, while success-dependent feedback raised them [38]. On a passing check, animate the thing that passed (the gap closing to its measured value), not the player.

12. **Do not add leaderboards or comparisons with other players.** Normative feedback is on the list of things that impede learning, especially for low achievers [17], and draws attention to the self [18].

13. **Animate only to show change, and give the player control of it.** Representational animation helps (d = 0.40) [35] and the benefit often comes from interactivity [36]. The slider demos qualify. Transitions between pages, an idle sprite or ambient particles do not, and static or looping decoration without pacing control is the condition where seductive details hurt most [33].

14. **Keep reading pages free of decoration.** Seductive details reduce learning (g = -0.33) [33], more so when learners cannot control pacing or when study time is limited [32, 33]. If a player sprite exists, it belongs on the desk, map or results screens, not beside lesson text, quiz items or the CSS editor.

15. **Apply "story or fact" to mean story that carries the principle.** The client's brief and the cost of the broken layout to that client are task context. Anecdotes that are interesting but do not teach the principle are seductive details, which damage learning by activating the wrong prior knowledge [31].

16. **Signal the link between demo and explanation.** Colour-link the measured property in the demo to the same number in the text; signalling helps low prior-knowledge learners most (r = 0.17) [34].

17. **Use contrasting cases for every before/after.** Pairs should differ in one property at a time so the player sees which feature carries the judgement [47]. Use the same rule for quiz items that ask which of two designs is better.

18. **Build the arcade as a perceptual learning module.** Many short classification items, immediate feedback, varied examples and mixed categories match the format that produced durable gains in Kellman's work [57, 58]. Expect uneven results across topics [58] and check per-topic accuracy.

19. **Precede the first client with a worked example, then fade.** Show an annotated fix with each step labelled by its principle [24]. Across the three clients, move from partially written CSS to a blank stylesheet [26]. Let players who pass gates first time skip the long example, because guidance redundant to what a learner knows can hurt [25].

20. **Keep the required rationale, as free text, prompted towards the principle.** Prompted self-explanation produces g = 0.55; menu-style prompts are the weakest form [50]. Prompt: "Which rule did you apply, and what did it change?". After submission, show the reference rationale for comparison. Use a sentence frame in early levels and remove it later [50].

21. **Design the hint ladder so it never ends in the answer.** Hints that terminate with the solution are abused [17], and gaming the system predicts low learning about as strongly as prior knowledge [61]. The last rung names the property and the principle, not the code. Require an edit between rungs, and offer a hint unprompted after repeated failed submissions for players who avoid help [62].

22. **Allow unlimited replay of challenges.** Unlimited access was one of the conditions under which simulation games outperformed comparison groups [53], and replay spaces practice [7].

23. **Spend production effort on learning mechanisms before polish.** Entertainment value did not predict learning [53], games were not more motivating than conventional instruction [51], and gamification's motivational effect was the less stable one [56]. The features with value-added evidence are coaching, self-explanation, pretraining, conversational voice and modality [54], and the game already contains most of them.

## Limits of this review

Most of the evidence comes from school, university and workplace training, not from adults playing a voluntary browser game, and almost none of it concerns visual design. The closest analogues are the painting-style studies [9, 10] and Kellman's perceptual learning work [57, 58]. Many effects are heterogeneous (I² = 84 in the testing-effect meta-analysis [3]), so moderators matter more than the pooled means. Several meta-analyses report publication bias or small subsets: the delayed-feedback advantage in Rowland rests on six effect sizes [3], and the productive-failure estimate of g = 0.87 is a bias-corrected projection, not an observed mean [45]. The evidence on "juice" is thin and recent [37, 38]. Where this review recommends something, the design should be checked against delayed player performance in the game itself.
